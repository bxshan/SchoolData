[ORG-1], mainly known as the [ORG-2], are awards for artistic and technical merit for the film industry. They are presented annually by [ORG-3] located in [GPE-1], [GPE-2], [GPE-3], in recognition of excellence in cinematic achievements as assessed by the [ORG-4]'s voting membership. [ORG-1] are regarded by many as the most prestigious, significant awards in the entertainment industry in [GPE-4] and worldwide. The [PERSON-1] statuette depicts a knight rendered in the Art Deco style.

The major award categories are presented during a live televised [GPE-5] ceremony that is typically held in February or March. It is the oldest worldwide entertainment awards ceremony. [ORG-5] were held in 1929, the second ceremony in 1930 was the first one broadcast by radio, and the 1953 ceremony was the first one televised. It is also the oldest of the four major annual [NORP-1] entertainment awards; its equivalents – the Emmy Awards for television, [ORG-6] for theater, and the Grammy Awards for music – are modeled after [ORG-7].

History 
The first [ORG-8] presentation was held on May 16, 1929, at a private dinner function at [ORG-9] with an audience of about 270 people.

The post-awards party was held at the Mayfair Hotel. The cost of guest tickets for that night's ceremony was $5 ($ at 2020 prices). Fifteen statuettes were awarded, honoring artists, directors and other participants in the film-making industry of the time, for their works during the 1927–28 period. The ceremony ran for 15 minutes.

For this first ceremony, winners were announced to the media three months earlier. For the second ceremony in 1930, and the rest of the first decade, the results were given to newspapers for publication at 11:00 pm on the night of the awards. In 1940, [ORG-10] announced the winners before the ceremony began; as a result, the following year the [ORG-4] started using a sealed envelope to reveal the names of the winners.

The term "Oscar" is a registered trademark of the [ORG-11]; however, in the [NORP-2] language, it is used generically to refer to any award or award ceremony, regardless of which field.

Nomination 
From 2004 to 2020, the Academy Award nomination results were announced to the public in mid-January. Prior to that, the results were announced in early February. In 2021, the nominees were announced in March. In 2022, the nominees were announced in early February for the first time since 2003.

Voters 
[ORG-60] (AMPAS), a professional honorary organization, maintains a voting membership of over 7,000 .

[ORG-4] membership is divided into different branches, with each representing a different discipline in film production. Actors constitute the largest voting bloc, numbering 1,311 members (22 percent) of the [ORG-4]'s composition. Votes have been certified by the auditing firm [ORG-61] (and its predecessor [PERSON-40]) since [ORG-62] in 1935. The firm mails the ballots of eligible nominees to members of the [ORG-4] in December to reflect the previous eligible year with a due date sometime in January of the next year, then tabulates the votes in a process that takes thousands of hours.

All AMPAS members must be invited to join by [ORG-63], on behalf of [ORG-64]. Membership eligibility may be achieved by a competitive nomination or a member may submit a name based on other significant contributions to the field of motion pictures.

New membership proposals are considered annually. The [ORG-4] does not publicly disclose its membership, although as recently as 2007 press releases have announced the names of those who have been invited to join. The 2007 release also stated that it has just under 6,000 voting members. While the membership had been growing, stricter policies have kept its size steady since then.

In 2012, the results of a study conducted by [ORG-10] were published describing the demographic breakdown of approximately 88% of [ORG-11]' voting membership. Of the 5,100+ active voters confirmed, 94% were [GPE-19], 77% were male, and 54% were found to be over the age of 60. 33% of voting members are former nominees (14%) and winners (19%).

In May 2011, the [ORG-4] sent a letter advising its 6,000 or so voting members that an online system for [PERSON-1] voting would be implemented in 2013.

Rules 
According to [PERSON-41] and 3 of the official [ORG-65], a film must open in the previous calendar year, from midnight at the start of January 1 to midnight at the end of December 31, in [GPE-20], [GPE-2], and play for seven consecutive days, to qualify (except for [ORG-66], Best Documentary Feature, and awards in short film categories). Additionally, the film must be shown at least three times on each day of its qualifying run, with at least one of the daily showings starting between 6 pm and 10 pm local time.

For example, the 2009 Best Picture winner, [ORG-67], was originally first released in 2008, but did not qualify for the 2008 awards, as it did not play its [PERSON-1]-qualifying run in [GPE-7] until mid-2009, thus qualifying for the 2009 awards. Foreign films must include English subtitles, and each country can submit only one film for consideration in [ORG-68] category per year.

Rule 2 states that a film must be feature-length, defined as a minimum of 40 minutes, except for short-subject awards, and it must exist either on a 35 mm or 70 mm film print or in 24 frame/s or 48 frame/s progressive [NORP-5] digital cinema format with a minimum projector resolution of 2048 by 1080 pixels. Since [ORG-69], presented in 2018, multi-part and limited series have been ineligible for [ORG-70] award. This followed the win of [GPE-21]: Made in [GPE-22], an eight-hour presentation that was screened in a limited release before being broadcast in five parts on [ORG-33] and [ORG-71], in that category in 2017. The [ORG-4]'s announcement of the new rule made no direct mention of that film.

[ORG-72] award does not require a [GPE-23] release. It requires the film to be submitted as its country's official selection.

[ORG-73] award requires either week-long releases in both [GPE-20] and any of the five boroughs of [GPE-24] during the previous calendar year, or a qualifying award at a competitive film festival from [ORG-74] list (regardless of any public exhibition or distribution), or submission in [ORG-68] category as its country's official selection. The qualifying theatrical runs must meet the same requirements as those for non-documentary films regarding numbers and times of screenings. Additionally, a film must have been reviewed by a critic from [ORG-75], [ORG-76] Out [GPE-14], [ORG-10], or [ORG-77].

Producers must submit an Official Screen Credits online form before the deadline; in case it is not submitted by the defined deadline, the film will be ineligible for [ORG-8] in any year. The form includes the production credits for all related categories. Then, each form is checked and put in a Reminder List of Eligible Releases.

Awards in short film categories (Best Documentary Short Subject, Best Animated Short Film, and Best Live Action Short Film) have noticeably different eligibility rules from most other competitive awards. First, the qualifying period for release does not coincide with a calendar year, instead covering one year starting on October 1, and ending on September 30 of the calendar year before the ceremony. Second, there are multiple methods of qualification. The main method is a week-long theatrical release in either [GPE-24] or [GPE-20] during the eligibility period. Films also can qualify by winning specified awards at one of several competitive film festivals designated by the [ORG-4], also without regard to prior public distribution. Finally, a film that is selected as a gold, silver, or bronze medal winner in an appropriate category of the immediately previous [ORG-78] is also eligible (Documentary category for that award, and [ORG-79], or International for the other awards). The requirements for the qualifying theatrical run are also different from those for other awards. Only one screening per day is required. For the Documentary award, the screening must start between noon and 10 pm local time; for other awards, no specific start time is required, but the film must appear in regular theater listings with dates and screening times.
In late December, ballots, and copies of [ORG-80] are mailed to around 6,000 active members. For most categories, members from each of the branches vote to determine the nominees only in their respective categories (i.e. only directors vote for directors, writers for writers, actors for actors, etc.). In the special case of [ORG-81], all voting members are eligible to select the nominees. In all major categories, a variant of the single transferable vote is used, with each member casting a ballot with up to five nominees (ten for Best Picture) ranked preferentially. In certain categories, including [ORG-82] Documentary and [ORG-83], nominees are selected by special screening committees made up of members from all branches.

In most categories, the winner is selected from among the nominees by plurality voting of all members. Since 2009, the Best Picture winner has been chosen by instant runoff voting. Since 2013, re-weighted range voting has been used to select the nominees for [ORG-84].

Film companies will spend as much as several million dollars on marketing to awards voters for a movie in the running for [ORG-81], in attempts to improve chances of receiving [ORG-2] and other movie awards conferred in [PERSON-1] season. The [ORG-4] enforces rules to limit overt campaigning by its members to try to eliminate excesses and prevent the process from becoming undignified. It has an awards czar on staff who advises members on allowed practices and levies penalties on offenders. For example, a producer of the 2009 Best Picture nominee The Hurt Locker was disqualified as a producer in the category when [PRON] contacted associates urging them to vote for [PRON] film and not another that was seen as the front-runner (The [ORG-85] eventually won).

Academy Screening Room 
The Academy Screening Room or Academy Digital Screening Room is a secure streaming platform which allows voting members of the [ORG-4] to view all eligible films (except, initially, those in the [ORG-86] category) in one place. It was introduced in 2019, for the 2020 Oscars, though DVD screeners and [ORG-4] in-person screenings were still provided. For films to be included on the platform, the [NORP-6] distributor must pay $12,500, including a watermarking fee, and a digital copy of the film to be prepared for streaming by the [ORG-4]. The platform can be accessed via [ORG-87] and [ORG-88] players. The watermarking process involved several video security firms, creating a forensic watermark and restricting the ability to take screenshots or screen recordings.

In 2021, for the 2022 [ORG-2], the [ORG-4] banned all physical screeners and in-person screenings, restricting official membership viewing to [ORG-89]. Films eligible in the Documentary and International categories were made available in different sections of the platform. Distributors can also pay an extra fee to add video featurettes to promote their films on the platform. The in-person screenings were said to be cancelled because of the [ORG-32] pandemic. Eligible films do not have to be added to the platform, but the [ORG-4] advertises them to voting members when they are.

Awards ceremonies

Telecast 

The major awards are presented at a live televised ceremony, commonly in late February or early March following the relevant calendar year, and six weeks after the announcement of the nominees. It is the culmination of the film awards season, which usually begins during November or December of the previous year. This is an elaborate extravaganza, with the invited guests walking up the red carpet in the creations of the most prominent fashion designers of the day. Black tie dress is the most common outfit for men, although fashion may dictate not wearing a bow-tie, and musical performers sometimes do not adhere to this (the artists who recorded the nominees for Best Original Song quite often perform those songs live at the awards ceremony, and the fact that they are performing is often used to promote the television broadcast).

[ORG-1] is the world's longest-running awards show televised live from the [GPE-23] to all time zones in North America and worldwide, and gathers billions of viewers elsewhere throughout the world. The [ORG-2] were first televised in 1953 by [ORG-90], which continued to broadcast the event until 1960, when [ORG-33] took over, televising the festivities (including the first color broadcast of the event in 1966) through 1970. [ORG-90] regained the rights for five years  then [ORG-33] resumed broadcast duties in 1976 and its current contract with the [ORG-4] runs through 2028. The [ORG-4] has also produced condensed versions of the ceremony for broadcast in international markets (especially those outside of the Americas) in more desirable local timeslots. The ceremony was broadcast live internationally for the first time via satellite since 1970, but only two [NORP-7] countries, [GPE-25] and [GPE-26], purchased the rights to air the broadcast. By that time, the television rights to [ORG-7] had been sold in 50 countries. A decade later, the rights were already being sold to 60 countries, and by 1984, the TV rights to the [ORG-91] were licensed in 76 countries.

The ceremonies were moved up from late March/early April to late February, since 2004, to help disrupt and shorten the intense lobbying and ad campaigns associated with [PERSON-1] season in the film industry. Another reason was because of the growing TV ratings success coinciding with the [ORG-92] basketball tournament, which would cut into [ORG-7] audience. (In 1976 and 1977, [ORG-33]'s regained [ORG-2] were moved from Tuesday to Monday and went directly opposite [ORG-90]'s [ORG-92] title game.) The earlier date is also to the advantage of [ORG-33], as it now usually occurs during the highly profitable and important February sweeps period. Some years, the ceremony is moved into the first Sunday of March to avoid a clash with [ORG-93]. Another reason for the move to late February and early March is also to avoid the awards ceremony occurring so close to the religious holidays of Passover and [PERSON-42], which for decades had been a grievance from members and the general public. Advertising is somewhat restricted, however, as traditionally no movie studios or competitors of official [ORG-14] sponsors may advertise during the telecast. The production of [ORG-7] telecast currently holds the distinction of winning the most [GPE-27] in history, with 47 wins and 195 nominations overall since that award's own launch in 1949.

After many years of being held on Mondays at 9:00 pm Eastern/6:00 p.m Pacific, since the 1999 ceremonies, it was moved to Sundays at 8:30 pm ET/5:30 pm PT. The reasons given for the move were that more viewers would tune in on Sundays, that [GPE-7] rush-hour traffic jams could be avoided, and an earlier start time would allow viewers on the East Coast to go to bed earlier. For many years the film industry opposed a Sunday broadcast because it would cut into the weekend box office. In 2010, the [ORG-4] contemplated moving the ceremony even further back into January, citing TV viewers' fatigue with the film industry's long awards season. However, such an accelerated schedule would dramatically decrease the voting period for its members, to the point where some voters would only have time to view the contending films streamed on their computers (as opposed to traditionally receiving the films and ballots in the mail). Furthermore, a January ceremony on Sunday would clash with [ORG-94] playoff games. In 2018, the [ORG-4] announced that the ceremony would be moved from late February to mid February beginning with [ORG-95] in 2020.

Criticism and controversies

Accusations of commercialism 
Due to the positive exposure and prestige of [ORG-7], many studios spend millions of dollars and hire publicists specifically to promote their films during what is typically called the "Oscar season." This has generated accusations of [ORG-7] being influenced more by marketing than by quality. [PERSON-54], an Academy Award-winning film director and former producer of the ceremony, expressed this sentiment at a conference in [GPE-14] in 2009, describing it as "the greatest promotion scheme that any industry ever devised for itself."

[PERSON-55], editor of [ORG-130]'s filmsite.org, has written of [ORG-131]

A recent technique that has been claimed to be used during the [PERSON-1] season is the whisper campaign. These campaigns are intended to spread negative perceptions of other movies nominated and are believed to be perpetrated by those that were involved in creating the movie. Examples of whisper campaigns include the allegations against Zero Dark Thirty suggesting that it justifies torture and the claim that [ORG-132] distorts history.

Accusations of bias 

Typical criticism of [ORG-133] is that among the winners and nominees there is an over-representation of romantic historical epics, biographical dramas, romantic dramedies and family melodramas, most of which are released in the [GPE-23] in the last three months of the calendar year. The [ORG-2] have been infamously known for selecting specific genres of movies to be awarded. The term "Oscar bait" was coined to describe such movies. This has led, at times, to more specific criticisms that the [ORG-4] is disconnected from the audience, e.g., by favoring "Oscar bait" over audience favorites or favoring historical melodramas over critically acclaimed movies that depict current life issues.

Allegations of a lack of diversity 
[ORG-1] have long received criticism over its lack of diversity among the nominees. This criticism is based on the statistics from [ORG-134] since 1929, which show that only 6.4% of academy award nominees have been non-white and since 1991, 11.2% of nominees have been non-white, with the rate of winners being even more polarizing. Due to a variety of reasons, including marketability and historical bans on interracial couples, a number of high-profile [ORG-2] have been given to yellowface portrayals, as well as performances of [NORP-8] characters rewritten for white characters. It took until 2023 for an [NORP-8] woman to win [ORG-135], when [PERSON-56] received the award for [PRON] performance in [ORG-136] All at Once. The 88th awards ceremony became the target of a boycott, popularized on social media with the hashtag #[PERSON-57], based on activists' perception that its all-white acting nominee list reflected bias. In response, the [ORG-4] initiated "historic" changes in membership by 2020. Some media critics claim the [ORG-4]'s efforts to address its purported racial, gender and national biases are merely distractions. By contrast, [ORG-137] already have multiple winners of [NORP-8] descent in leading actress categories. Some question whether the [ORG-4]'s definition of "merit" is just or empowering for [NORP-9].

Miscategorization of actors 

The [ORG-4] doesn't have any rules for how to categorize whether a performance is leading or supporting, and it is up to the discretion of the studios whether a given performance is submitted for either [ORG-138] or [ORG-139]. This has led situations where a film has two or more co-leads, and one of these is submitted in a supporting category to avoid the two leads competing against each other, and to increase the film's chances of winning. This practice has been derisively called "category fraud". For example, [PERSON-58] was nominated for [ORG-140] (2015), despite [PRON] having a comparable amount of screentime to [ORG-141], who was nominated for [ORG-26]. Another example is Once Upon a Time in [GPE-5] (2019), where [PERSON-59] was nominated for and won [ORG-25], even though [PRON] played an equally important role to [ORG-24] nominee [PERSON-60]. In both these cases, critics argued that [ORG-142], the studio behind the former film, had placed someone who was actually a leading actor or actress into the supporting categories to avoid them competing against their co-lead.

Symbolism or sentimentalization 
Acting prizes in certain years have been criticized for not recognizing superior performances so much as being awarded for personal popularity, to make up for a "snub" for a work that proved in time to be more popular or renowned than the one awarded, or presented as a "career honor" to recognize a distinguished nominee's entire body of work.
